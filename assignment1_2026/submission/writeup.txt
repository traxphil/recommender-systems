### Core Ideas of my Approach - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

Early on, I realized that I struggled with overfitting my models and Surprise has no real way of doing early stopping. This is why I
wanted to use Pytorch, with the motivation to have more overall control and insignt into what is happening. This would also allow me to 
apply adaptive learning rates and have more control overall when it comes to fine-tuning my code. But this came with significantly more
work than I initially expected. The main difficulty came with setting up the whole infrastructure, handlers, and architecture of my
solution approach, because I quickly realized that I would lose any sense of overview, if I just wrote everything into a single file.

This is why this whole project repository has grown to the size that it currently has. I have followed a strict approach of modularization
for my individual responsibilities in my code (I'm also taking CSE 210 at the moment and this class also really teaches the importance of
modularization and separation of concerns. I have applied these ideas in this project extensively).

I also want to put a large emphasis on mathematical understanding of the models and ideas I apply in this project. Using pytorch also makes
it significantly more important to have a closer look at the actual math behind the models. This level of insight would have been almost 
entirely obstructed if I stuck with the SciKit Surprise models, even though it might have been significantly easier. In all of the notebooks
I have included the mathematical descriptions of the models. This whole project quickly grew larger than I initially expected and I want to
be able to use the code I developed here in the future as well. I hope this repository will be able to serve me as a reference for future
use of pytorch and other machine learning projects. Therefore, this project is more or less also an investment into my future knowledge base
going beyond just the scope of the class. 

This writeup will also reference the sources of where I found my ideas and how I have included them into the implementations for the 
individual tasks. For all of the implementations, I opted to use ensemble models as this generally seems to be a good approach for building
competitive recommender systems that generalize well. I would have probably preferred to keep the implementation a bit simpler and smaller
but the pressure of the leaderboard made it almost necessary, to pursue more advanced solutions. 

I have also discussed my ideas with Claude to come up with better ideas, improve existing approaches, or identify shortcomings in my ideas. 
But this primarily served the purpose of guiding me through the material and helping me out when I was stuck. I have no intention of just
letting an LLM write my code as I personally also see absolutely no benefit in doing that. But I have to admit dhat it definitely was an amazing 
help, especially when I went for the more complex models or repetitive notation tasks (e.g. for the formulas).



### Repository Structure - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

All of the files related to the assignment can be found under the /assignment1_2026 folder. The core components of my submission are all
included in /submission. 

-   /lib contains all of the additional helber functions (e.g. data processing, pytorch training, custom data types, etc.)

-   /lib/models contains all the models that were used by the tasks

-   /lib/config contains the model settings and their hyperparameters

-   /output contains the written prediction files (given that the repo is public and the scoring is competitive, I have excluded them from the repo)

-   assignment1.py contains the main functions for the training of the model

-   the taskX_ensemble.ipynb notebooks handle the entire pipeline for each task -> so to reproduce the results one only has to run the notebooks
